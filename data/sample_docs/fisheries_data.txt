FISHERIES DATA AND STOCK ASSESSMENT REFERENCE GUIDE
====================================================

1. GLOBAL FISHERIES OVERVIEW
------------------------------
Global marine capture fisheries production reached approximately 80–90 million
tonnes per year in recent years. According to the FAO (Food and Agriculture
Organization of the United Nations) State of World Fisheries and Aquaculture
(SOFIA) 2022 report:

- 57.3% of marine fish stocks are fished at biologically sustainable levels
- 35.4% are overfished (above maximum sustainable yield)
- 7.2% are underfished

Top fishing nations by volume (marine capture, million tonnes):
1. China: ~15 million tonnes
2. Indonesia: ~7.0 million tonnes
3. Peru: ~5.9 million tonnes
4. Russia: ~5.1 million tonnes
5. United States: ~4.9 million tonnes

Top commercially important species globally:
- Anchoveta (Engraulis ringens): largest single-species catch (~7–8 Mt/year)
- Alaska pollock (Gadus chalcogrammus): ~3.5 Mt/year
- Atlantic herring (Clupea harengus): ~1.8 Mt/year
- Blue whiting (Micromesistius poutassou): ~1.5 Mt/year
- Atlantic mackerel (Scomber scombrus): ~1.0 Mt/year

2. STOCK ASSESSMENT METHODS
-----------------------------
Scientific stock assessments evaluate the status of fish populations and
form the basis for fisheries management advice.

Key assessment approaches:
- Surplus production models (e.g., Schaefer model, Fox model): use total
  catch and effort data to estimate stock biomass and fishing mortality
- Age-structured models (VPA, SAM, SS3): use catch-at-age data and
  surveys to estimate population dynamics parameters
- CPUE (Catch Per Unit Effort): standardised index of stock abundance;
  declining CPUE indicates stock depletion
- Acoustic surveys: hydroacoustic surveys provide direct biomass estimates
  for pelagic species (herring, mackerel, sprat)

Reference points used in management:
- MSY (Maximum Sustainable Yield): theoretical maximum catch maintainable
  indefinitely from a stock
- Bmsy: stock biomass at which MSY is achieved
- Fmsy: fishing mortality rate at which MSY is achieved
- Blim: minimum stock size below which recruitment may be impaired
- Bpa: precautionary biomass reference point (typically 1.4 × Blim)
- Flim: fishing mortality causing stock collapse
- Fpa: precautionary fishing mortality (typically = Fmsy or lower)

Stock status indicators:
- B/Bmsy > 1.0: stock is above target biomass (healthy)
- B/Bmsy 0.5–1.0: stock is depleted but recovering
- B/Bmsy < 0.5: stock is severely depleted
- F/Fmsy < 1.0: fishing mortality below target (sustainable)
- F/Fmsy > 1.0: overfishing is occurring

3. MAJOR COMMERCIAL FISH SPECIES – PROFILES
---------------------------------------------

ATLANTIC COD (Gadus morhua):
- Distribution: North Atlantic from eastern North America to northwestern Europe
- Maximum size: ~150 cm, ~40 kg; typical commercial size 50–100 cm
- Age at maturity: 2–4 years depending on population
- Spawning: January–April; spawning aggregations form in specific areas
- Diet: carnivorous; feeds on capelin, herring, invertebrates
- Status (Northeast Arctic stock): above Bpa; F below Fmsy — healthy
- Status (Celtic Sea stock): below Blim — critically depleted; emergency
  management measures in effect
- Quota: Northeast Arctic cod TAC = 453,000 tonnes (2024)

ATLANTIC HERRING (Clupea harengus):
- Distribution: North Atlantic and Baltic Sea
- Maximum size: ~45 cm, ~700 g
- Spawning: varies by stock; spring or autumn spawners
- Highly migratory; forms large schools
- Status (Norwegian Spring Spawning stock): above Bpa; sustainable
- TAC (Norwegian Spring Spawning, 2024): 316,000 tonnes shared among
  Norway, Iceland, Faroe Islands, Russia, EU

EUROPEAN HAKE (Merluccius merluccius):
- Distribution: Northeast Atlantic, Mediterranean Sea
- Maximum size: ~140 cm; maximum age ~20 years
- Age at maturity: 2–3 years
- Deep-water species; migrates vertically; adults found at 200–1000 m depth
- Status (Northern stock): recovering; currently above Bpa
- TAC (Northern stock, 2024): 103,700 tonnes

BLUEFIN TUNA (Thunnus thynnus):
- Distribution: entire Atlantic Ocean and Mediterranean Sea; highly migratory
- Maximum size: ~300 cm, ~680 kg; maximum age ~40 years
- Age at maturity: 8–12 years
- Trans-oceanic migrations between feeding and spawning grounds
- Status (Eastern stock): recovering; biomass increasing due to quota cuts
- Status (Western stock): still below Bmsy; under rebuilding plan

ATLANTIC SALMON (Salmo salar):
- Anadromous species: spawns in freshwater, adults live in ocean
- Populations declining across most of range due to habitat loss,
  overexploitation, sea lice from aquaculture, and climate change
- Wild salmon catch subject to strict limitations; most commercial
  production from aquaculture (Norway produces ~1.4 Mt/year)

4. FISHING GEAR TYPES AND SELECTIVITY
---------------------------------------
Different gear types have different selectivity characteristics and
ecosystem impacts:

Bottom trawl:
- Targets demersal species (cod, haddock, flatfishes, Nephrops)
- High bycatch potential; bottom contact causes seafloor disturbance
- Mesh size regulations determine size selectivity

Pelagic trawl:
- Targets mid-water and surface schooling species (herring, mackerel, sprat)
- Lower bycatch than bottom trawls; minimal bottom contact

Longline:
- Fixed gear with baited hooks; high target species selectivity
- Concerns: seabird and sea turtle bycatch; requires bird-scaring devices
  and circle hooks to mitigate

Purse seine:
- Encircles schools of pelagic fish with a net wall, then closes the bottom
- Very efficient for schooling species (tuna, sardine, herring)
- Can attract non-target species; FAD (fish aggregating device) use
  increases bycatch of juvenile tunas and non-target species

Gillnet:
- Passive gear; fish are caught by gilling or tangling in the net
- Size selective based on mesh size; risk of ghost fishing if lost
- Cetacean bycatch is a major concern; acoustic deterrents (pingers) required

Pot/Trap:
- Highly selective passive gear for crustaceans (lobster, crab, Nephrops)
- Low ecosystem impact; good survival rates for non-target species

5. AQUACULTURE PRODUCTION DATA
--------------------------------
Global aquaculture (freshwater and marine) contributes approximately
50% of all fish consumed by humans:

Marine aquaculture key species (global production):
- Pacific cupped oyster (Crassostrea gigas): ~5.5 Mt/year
- Atlantic salmon (Salmo salar): ~2.7 Mt/year
- Sea bass & sea bream (Mediterranean species): ~0.5 Mt/year
- Shrimp (various Penaeus species): ~6 Mt/year (largely brackish)
- Mussels (Mytilus species): ~2.1 Mt/year

Aquaculture sustainability concerns:
- Feed conversion ratios (FCR): salmon requires ~1.2–1.5 kg feed per kg
  of live weight gain when fed modern low-fishmeal diets
- Fish meal and fish oil: ingredient sustainability; shift toward
  plant-based and novel protein sources (insect meal, single-cell protein)
- Sea lice (Lepeophtheirus salmonis): major parasite in salmon farming;
  requires integrated pest management
- Escapes: genetic interactions with wild populations; require containment

6. FISHERIES ECONOMIC DATA
----------------------------
Economic value of global marine fisheries:
- First-sale value of marine capture production: ~$130–150 billion/year
- Employment: ~600 million people depend on fisheries and aquaculture
  for their livelihoods (directly and indirectly)
- Food security: fish provides > 20% of per-capita animal protein intake
  for 3.3 billion people worldwide

Economic reference points in management:
- MEY (Maximum Economic Yield): fishing effort level that maximises
  long-term profit; typically 70–80% of MSY effort level
- Open access equilibrium: without management, effort expands until
  economic profits are driven to zero (dissipates economic rent)
- ITQ systems (Individual Transferable Quotas): market-based mechanisms
  that can improve economic efficiency and reduce overcapacity

7. ECOSYSTEM-BASED FISHERIES MANAGEMENT (EBFM)
------------------------------------------------
EBFM considers fisheries within the broader ecosystem context:

- Trophic interactions: account for predator-prey relationships when
  setting harvest levels; avoid removal of key forage fish species
  (anchovies, capelin, sand lance) that are critical food sources for
  whales, seabirds, and larger predatory fish
- Habitat protection: protect essential fish habitat (EFH) from
  destructive fishing practices, especially nursery areas and spawning grounds
- Biodiversity: maintain community diversity; avoid targeted removal of
  low-productivity, long-lived species
- Climate adaptation: incorporate climate projections into stock assessments
  and harvest control rules to improve long-term stock sustainability
- Cumulative impacts: consider all human pressures on ecosystems
  (fishing, pollution, offshore energy, shipping) in management plans
